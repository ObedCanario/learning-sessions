Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter seven: the brave person, the reckless person, and the coward.

Here is today's central idea, expressed in a single sentence: courage is not the absence of fear, but fear handled correctly. [pause 1.5]

So here is what to listen for. Aristotle identifies the different ways fear can go wrong, and the list is considerably longer than you might expect. Then he describes the reckless person, and you may recognize someone you know. Finally, notice how differently brave and reckless people behave before and during danger.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-5}}

Let's slow down on that list, because it functions as a remarkably powerful diagnostic tool for examining your own reactions.

You can fear the wrong thing, fear it too much or too little, fear it in the wrong way, and fear it at the wrong time. That's at least four different dials, and the brave person has all of them set correctly.

Think of a sound engineer at a mixing desk. Good sound isn't about turning one knob all the way up or all the way down. It's about setting each knob to exactly the right level for this particular song. Courage works the same way. The brave person fears the right things, for the right reasons, in the right way, at the right time.

And here's the first principle underneath. Aristotle says even the brave person feels fear. Courage does not mean feeling nothing. It means feeling the right amount, and then acting well anyway. [pause 2]

{{read 6-8}}

Notice what Aristotle says about the person who fears absolutely nothing, not even earthquakes or enormous ocean waves. He doesn't call that person brave. He calls them a kind of madman, or someone who has lost the ability to feel.

The image of the Celts charging into the sea with weapons to fight the waves makes the point beautifully. That isn't courage. It's a failure to understand what a wave is. Genuine courage requires an accurate understanding of danger. [pause 2]

{{read 9-11}}

This is the most revealing psychological portrait in the chapter, so let's study it carefully.

The reckless person is often a show-off, imitating courage because they want to look brave. They talk loudly before the danger arrives, and then, when it actually arrives, they disappear.

Aristotle sums up the difference in two beautiful lines. Reckless people are eager beforehand and back off once they're in it. Brave people are calm beforehand and sharp when the moment comes.

Let's make it modern. Picture two people preparing for a hard negotiation. One spends the week bragging about how they'll dominate the other side, and then caves in the first ten minutes. The other spends the week quietly preparing, says very little, and then holds firm when the pressure arrives. The first is recklessness wearing courage as a costume. The second is the real thing. [pause 2]

So here is a line worth writing down and remembering: the reckless are loud before and absent during, while the brave are quiet before and present during. [pause 2]

And notice what Aristotle says about the coward. A coward is hopeless, while boldness is a sign of hope. At its root, courage is a form of hope: the belief that facing the difficult thing is worth it.

{{read 12-13}}

Aristotle closes the chapter with a difficult truth, and it deserves to be heard carefully and compassionately. Running from pain, however great, is not courage, even when the escape looks dramatic. Courage faces difficulty for the sake of something good. It does not flee from it.

So let's put the chapter together.

Courage sits in the middle, between the coward who fears too much and the reckless person who fears too little. [pause 1.5]

Fear can go wrong in many ways: the wrong object, the wrong amount, the wrong manner, the wrong time. [pause 1.5]

The reckless person is loud before and absent during, while the brave person is calm before and ready during. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Think of something difficult you have coming up. Instead of talking about it, prepare for it quietly. Then notice how different you feel when the moment actually arrives.

In chapter eight, Aristotle examines five things that look like courage but aren't. One of them may describe the way you've been brave until now.

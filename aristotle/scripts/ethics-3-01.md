Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter one: willing and unwilling actions.

Here is the question for today, and it is a bigger question than it first appears. When is something genuinely your doing, and not the responsibility of your boss, your parents, your circumstances, or the weather? [pause 1.5]

Your entire sense of personal responsibility depends on the answer. If you get it wrong in one direction, you will carry guilt for things you never controlled. If you get it wrong in the other direction, you will quietly excuse yourself for things you absolutely did.

So here is what to listen for. Aristotle is going to give you exactly two situations in which an action is not really yours. Count them carefully, and then notice how much territory is left over, because everything left over belongs to you.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-2}}

Let's slow down right there and ask a simple but important question: why would Aristotle begin a serious discussion of character with something as ordinary as praise and blame?

Take it down to the foundation. Praise and blame only make sense when an action comes from a person who could have acted differently. Nobody congratulates the rain for watering the garden, and nobody blames a falling rock for the damage it causes. We only hold a source responsible. So before we can talk seriously about becoming a good person, we have to establish what actually originates in you. That is the foundation, and everything else in this book is constructed on top of it. [pause 2]

{{read 3-4}}

Here is the root of the word "forced." The cause is outside you, and you contribute nothing. Pay attention to that phrase, "you contribute nothing." When the wind pushes you across the street, your legs are not doing the work. When someone drags you through a doorway, your will is not involved. You are being transported, like a package on a delivery truck.

Notice how demanding that definition really is, because it does not say "I didn't feel like it," and it does not say "I was under enormous pressure." It says, in effect, "my body moved, but I was not the one moving it." That is a very small circle, and Aristotle deliberately keeps it small.

{{read 5-7}}

This is where the chapter becomes genuinely interesting, so stay with me, because the distinction Aristotle draws here applies to nearly every difficult decision you will ever make.

Consider the sailors in the storm who throw the cargo overboard, and ask whether they would ever do that on a calm afternoon. Certainly not. So in one sense, nobody wants to do it. But in the middle of the storm, at that particular moment, who actually opens their hands and releases the cargo? The sailors do, with their own arms and their own decision.

Here is the first principle underneath it. An action always happens in a specific moment, never in general. In general, you would never throw away valuable goods. But nobody lives in general. You live right here, in this storm, facing this decision, and in this storm, you chose.

Let me translate that into modern life. Suppose you accept a job you don't particularly love because the rent is due next week. You might tell me, "I had no choice." But you did have a choice. It was a difficult choice between imperfect options, and you selected the better one. That is not being forced. That is deciding under pressure, and the decision belongs to you. [pause 2]

And here is why that is actually good news. If the decision is yours, then you can own it, learn from it, and make a better one next time. Excuses take away your discomfort, but they also take away your power. [pause 2]

{{read 8-11}}

There is a hard truth inside that passage. Some actions should never be taken, regardless of the pressure, and the story of Alcmaeon illustrates it. He killed his own mother and then claimed he had been forced into it by his father's curses. Aristotle simply calls that excuse ridiculous.

Let's unpack why. Pressure can explain a great deal of human behavior, but it cannot excuse everything. There is a line, and each of us needs to know where our own line is before the storm arrives, because in the middle of the storm, clear thinking becomes extremely difficult. As Aristotle observes, it is even harder to stick with your decision once you have made it.

So here is the practical lesson, and it is one of the most valuable in the entire chapter. Decide where your lines are on a calm day. [pause 2]

{{read 12-13}}

This idea is worth writing down. Pleasure is not a force. If pleasure could force you, then every action you have ever taken would be forced, because there is always some attraction pulling you toward one thing or another.

And look at the clever psychological test he offers: when someone is genuinely forced, they experience pain. When someone acts for pleasure, they experience enjoyment. You cannot be dragged into something against your will while enjoying the ride.

Then he catches all of us in a familiar little trick. When things go well, we say, "Look at what I accomplished." When things go badly, we say, "The temptation was simply too strong." Aristotle's point is that you cannot have it both ways. If the good actions are yours, then the bad ones are yours as well. [pause 2]

{{read 14-16}}

Now we arrive at the second exception, which Aristotle describes as acting without knowing, or acting out of ignorance.

Aristotle adds a beautiful psychological detail here, and that detail is regret. If you caused harm by accident and you genuinely regret it, you acted unwillingly. If you caused harm by accident and you don't care at all, he places you in a separate category. Why would he do that? Because your reaction afterward reveals what was in your heart all along. Regret is evidence that you never wanted the harm.

Then comes a sharp distinction that is easy to miss. Acting because of ignorance is different from acting while ignorant. The drunk person really doesn't know what he's doing, that's true. But the alcohol is the reason, and he chose the alcohol. The not-knowing arrived after his own decision, so he cannot hide behind it.

{{read 17-18}}

This is the core of the entire chapter, so let's go slowly and examine it carefully.

There are two very different kinds of not knowing. The first kind is not knowing what is right, as in, "I didn't realize it was wrong to cheat." That does not excuse you. In fact, Aristotle says that kind of ignorance is precisely what makes a person bad.

The second kind is not knowing the particular facts of the situation, as in, "I didn't know the gun was loaded," or "I had no idea the medicine would react that way." That kind of ignorance can genuinely excuse you.

Why does he draw the line there? Because understanding right from wrong is your responsibility as a human being, and nobody else can do that work for you. But the specific facts of a particular moment can be hidden from anyone, including a thoroughly good person.

Then he hands you a checklist of six details: who is acting, what is being done, to whom, with what instrument, for what result, and in what manner. Think of it like a pilot's checklist before takeoff. If one of those items was wrong and you had no reasonable way of knowing, the mistake is not a mark on your character. [pause 2]

{{read 19-21}}

The examples are vivid, so let's translate a couple of them into modern terms.

The spear with the safety cap is like a training sword, or a prop gun that someone believes is harmless. The pumice stone is like a foam brick on a film set that turns out to be a real brick. The friendly sparring match that goes wrong tells the same story. In each case, the person knew they were acting, but one important fact was hidden from them.

Notice which facts matter most to Aristotle: what you were actually doing, and what it would eventually lead to. Those two considerations carry the most weight in determining responsibility.

{{read 22-23}}

So let's put the whole chapter together.

An action fails to be yours in only two situations. Either it was truly forced, with the cause entirely outside you and nothing contributed by you. Or you were unaware of a key fact of the situation, and you regret what happened. [pause 2]

Everything else belongs to you. Decisions made under pressure belong to you, and so do the things you do in anger or for pleasure. [pause 2]

And here is the part I appreciate most. Aristotle says feelings are just as human as reason. Your anger and your desire are not invaders from somewhere outside you. They are part of you, which means they can be trained, shaped, and improved, exactly the way your thinking can be. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. The next time you hear yourself say "I had no choice," pause and ask an honest question. Was I carried like a package, or did I choose between difficult options? If you chose, say it out loud: "I chose this." Then notice how different it feels to take ownership.

In chapter two, Aristotle goes one step deeper. Not every willing action is a choice, so what exactly is a choice? The answer will change how you think about your own decisions.

Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter three: thinking things through.

Here is today's question, and it is more practical than it might initially sound: what is actually worth deliberating about? [pause 1.5]

Most of us spend enormous mental energy on things we cannot change: the economy, the weather, other people's opinions, and things that happened years ago. Aristotle offers a remarkably clean filter for where your thinking should go, and if you take it seriously, it can return hours of your life to you.

So here is what to listen for. First, the list of things nobody sensibly deliberates about. Second, the claim that we think about the route rather than the destination. Third, his elegant idea that the first step is the last thing you figure out.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-4}}

Let's take that list down to its foundation. Aristotle identifies four causes of everything that happens: nature, necessity, luck, and human action. Only the last one is up to you.

The stars, the seasons, mathematical facts, the rainfall, and buried treasure all belong to the first three. You can study them, admire them, and prepare for them, but you cannot deliberate about them, because deliberation means deciding what to do, and none of those things are yours to do.

Even human affairs are not all yours. His example is a Spartan who sits down to plan the government of a distant nation. Today, that is the person who spends the evening furious about decisions being made on the other side of the world, and who neglects the decisions waiting in their own kitchen.

Here is the first principle. Deliberate only where your effort can change the result. Everything else deserves your attention, perhaps, but not your deliberation. [pause 2]

{{read 5}}

This passage contains a quiet insight about uncertainty. We do not deliberate about spelling, because the rules are fixed and there is nothing to decide. We deliberate about medicine, business, and sailing, because the outcome is uncertain and our judgment actually matters.

Notice his final sentence, too. On the biggest questions, we ask others for help, because we don't trust ourselves to decide alone. That is not weakness, it is wisdom. The bigger and more uncertain the decision, the more valuable a second set of eyes becomes. [pause 2]

{{read 6-7}}

This is the heart of the chapter, so let's go slowly and examine each piece of the reasoning.

The doctor does not ask whether to heal, because healing is the whole point of being a doctor. The doctor asks how. The goal is taken as given, and all the thinking goes into the route.

Then comes the brilliant part of Aristotle's analysis, which is that you begin with the goal and work systematically backward. "To be healthy, I need to lose weight. To lose weight, I need to change what I eat. To change what I eat, I need to change what's in my kitchen. To change what's in my kitchen, I need to go shopping with a list." And there it is, at the very end of your thinking, the very first thing you actually do: write the list.

That is what Aristotle means when he says that what comes last in the planning comes first in the doing. It is like solving a maze by starting at the exit and tracing your way back to the entrance. [pause 2]

So here is the lesson to write down. If you don't know your first step, you haven't finished thinking yet. Keep tracing backward until you reach something you can do today. [pause 2]

{{read 8-10}}

Two practical observations are hiding inside those paragraphs, and both of them deserve your attention.

First, when the trail leads to something impossible, a sensible person stops and looks for a different route. That is not quitting. It is refusing to waste effort on a road that doesn't exist.

Second, notice his phrase about bread. We don't deliberate about whether this is bread or whether it is baked enough. We look, and we know. If you had to deliberate about everything, you would never finish a single decision. Part of wisdom is knowing what needs thinking and what simply needs looking.

{{read 11-13}}

So let's put the chapter together.

We deliberate only about things that are up to us and can actually be done. [pause 1.5]

We deliberate about the route, not about the destination itself. [pause 1.5]

We trace backward from the goal until we reach the first step, and that first step is the last thing we discover. [pause 2]

And then Aristotle gives his definition, which is worth memorizing: choice is a thought-out desire for something within our power. Once you have reasoned your way to a decision, your desire follows that decision. That is how a disciplined life is built, one deliberate decision at a time. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Pick one goal that has been sitting on your list for too long. Trace it backward, step by step, until you reach something you can do in the next twenty-four hours. Then do that one thing.

In chapter four, Aristotle asks what we wish for in the first place. Do we wish for what is actually good, or only for what seems good to us? His answer explains why pleasure fools so many people.

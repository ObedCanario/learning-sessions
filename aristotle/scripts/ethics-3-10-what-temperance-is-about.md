Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter ten: what temperance is about.

We now move from courage to a second virtue: temperance, which simply means self-control with pleasures. [pause 1.5]

Here's the question. Is self-control about every pleasure? Can you be self-indulgent about learning, music, or beautiful paintings? Aristotle says no. Temperance has a very specific territory, and once you see it clearly, you'll understand why it matters so much.

So here is what to listen for. Aristotle will walk through the senses one at a time, almost like a scientist ruling out variables. Notice which senses he excludes, and listen for the strange story of the glutton and the crane.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-4}}

Let's take that first distinction down to its foundation.

There are pleasures of the mind, like learning, honor, and good conversation, and pleasures of the body. Aristotle says temperance isn't about the mind's pleasures at all. Someone who loves learning too much might have a problem, but we don't call it self-indulgence. Someone who talks too much is a gossip, not a glutton.

Why does that matter? Because it tells you that temperance isn't a general suspicion of enjoyment. Aristotle isn't against pleasure. He is pointing to one specific area where pleasure tends to get out of control. [pause 2]

{{read 5-9}}

Now look at the method. Sight is excluded, and so is hearing. Smell counts only indirectly, when it reminds you of something you crave.

Then come the animal examples, and they are wonderfully clarifying. The dog doesn't enjoy the smell of the rabbit for its own sake. The smell is just a signal that dinner is nearby. The lion doesn't love the sound of the cow. The sound is a dinner bell.

Here is the first principle. For an animal, sight, sound, and smell are only messengers. The real pleasure is at the end of the message, in eating. Aristotle is showing you the animal part of human pleasure, so you can recognize it in yourself. [pause 2]

{{read 10-11}}

This is the most memorable image in the chapter, so let's unpack it.

The glutton wished his throat were longer than a crane's neck. Why would anyone want that? Because he wasn't enjoying the flavor, which is the job of taste. He was enjoying the sensation of the food going down, which is touch. A longer throat would simply mean more of that sensation.

Aristotle's point is subtle but powerful. A wine expert or a chef genuinely uses taste. They notice, compare, and appreciate. The self-indulgent person barely notices the flavor at all. What they crave is the physical act of consuming.

Let's make it modern. Think about eating a whole bag of chips in front of the television. By the end, you can barely remember tasting them. That's the glutton's throat. It isn't about enjoyment. It's about consumption. [pause 2]

{{read 12-13}}

So here's why self-indulgence is criticized so strongly. It's tied to touch, the sense we share most widely with every other animal. When those pleasures become the center of your life, you are living from the animal part of yourself rather than the distinctly human part.

So let's put the chapter together.

Temperance isn't about pleasures of the mind, or about sight, hearing, or most of smell. [pause 1.5]

It's about the pleasures we share with animals, mainly touch, as in eating, drinking, and sex. [pause 1.5]

And the self-indulgent person doesn't really savor anything. They simply want more of the sensation, like the glutton who wished for a longer throat. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. At one meal each day, eat slowly enough to genuinely taste your food. Notice the difference between savoring and simply consuming. That difference is the doorway to temperance.

In chapter eleven, Aristotle describes the temperate person and the self-indulgent person side by side, and shows why the self-indulgent person ends up suffering for the sake of pleasure.

Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter five: we are responsible for our character.

This may be the most important chapter in the entire book, so let me introduce it carefully. [pause 1.5]

Most people will readily admit that their actions are their own. But their character feels different, as though it were something they were simply handed, like their height or the color of their eyes. "That's just the way I am." Aristotle takes that sentence apart piece by piece, and what he leaves behind is both demanding and deeply encouraging.

So here is what to listen for. First, the claim that whatever is up to us to do is also up to us not to do. Second, the image of a thrown stone. Third, the "inner eye" objection, and how Aristotle answers it.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-3}}

Let's take that argument down to its foundation, because it is simpler than it sounds.

If something is genuinely in your power, then it is in your power in both directions. If you can choose to speak, you can choose to stay silent. If you can choose to help, you can choose not to help. Power always cuts both ways.

So if good actions are up to you, then bad actions must be up to you as well. And since being a good or bad person simply means habitually doing good or bad actions, being good or bad is ultimately up to you. [pause 2]

{{read 4-5}}

Look at the old saying Aristotle quotes: "No one is bad on purpose, and no one is happy by accident." He agrees with the second half and rejects the first.

Here is the evidence he offers, and it is the evidence of how everyone actually behaves. Every society punishes bad conduct and honors good conduct. Nobody tries to persuade you not to feel hungry, because hunger is not up to you. But everyone tries to persuade you to be honest, because honesty is. The way people treat each other reveals what they genuinely believe about responsibility, whatever theories they may claim to hold. [pause 2]

{{read 6-8}}

This is the engine room of the entire chapter, so let's slow down and examine the mechanism carefully.

Aristotle says the careless person made themselves careless by living carelessly. The unfair person made themselves unfair by repeatedly cheating. Character is not something that happens to you. It accumulates from what you repeatedly do, the way fitness accumulates from what you repeatedly do in the gym.

Think about an athlete preparing for a competition. Nobody is surprised that the person who trains every morning becomes strong. Aristotle says only a completely senseless person would fail to see that character works exactly the same way. Your daily actions are your training program, whether you intend them to be or not. [pause 2]

So here is the line to write down and remember: you are not only what you do, because over time, you become what you repeatedly do. [pause 2]

{{read 9}}

Now comes what may be the most memorable image in the entire book, so let's unpack it carefully and completely.

When a stone is still in your hand, you have complete control over it. You can throw it, keep it, or put it down. But once it leaves your hand, it follows its own path, and no amount of wishing will bring it back. Throwing it, however, was entirely your decision.

Habits work the same way. In the early stages, when you first skip the workout, tell the small lie, or reach for the extra drink, you have complete control. But after a thousand repetitions, the habit has momentum of its own. You can't simply wish it away, just as a sick person can't wish themselves healthy.

Here is the honest part, and the hopeful part, together. The honest part is that you are responsible even for the habits that now feel automatic, because you released the stone. The hopeful part is that the same mechanism works in your favor. Every good action you repeat today is a stone thrown in the right direction, and it will carry you further than you can imagine. [pause 2]

{{read 10}}

Aristotle reinforces the point with the body. Nobody blames a person born with poor eyesight, but we do hold someone responsible for damage caused by years of heavy drinking. We distinguish between what nature handed us and what we did to ourselves. If we apply that distinction to the body, consistency requires that we apply it to character as well.

{{read 11-13}}

This objection is the strongest one Aristotle considers in the chapter, so let's lay it out plainly and fairly.

The objector says: "Everyone pursues whatever seems good to them, but what seems good depends on the kind of person you already are. So you are trapped. Your character determines your vision, and your vision determines your choices."

The image Aristotle uses here is an inner eye, a built-in sense of what is truly good. If that eye were simply given to us at birth, like natural eyesight, then a person with good vision would be lucky and a person with poor vision would be unlucky, and nobody would deserve praise or blame for anything.

And then he springs the trap on the objection itself. If that were true, it would apply to good people just as much as bad people. Virtue would be no more our own doing than vice. You can't claim credit for your good qualities while disowning your bad ones.

{{read 14-17}}

So here is Aristotle's conclusion, put plainly. Even if some of your starting point came from nature, you are still partly responsible for how the world looks to you, because your repeated choices have shaped your vision. [pause 2]

And notice the final distinction, which is subtle and very wise. You control your actions from beginning to end, as long as you know the facts. But your character grows like an illness that creeps up slowly, or like a tree that grows too gradually to watch. You never notice a single day's change, and yet the change is real, and it is yours. [pause 2]

So let's put the whole chapter together.

Whatever is up to you to do is also up to you not to do. [pause 1.5]

Character is built from repeated actions, the way strength is built from repeated training. [pause 1.5]

Once a habit is formed, it's like a thrown stone that you can't simply call back. But you threw it, so it's yours. [pause 1.5]

And even the way the world looks to you is partly your own creation. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Pick one small habit you would like to have a year from now, and do the smallest possible version of it every single day this week. You are not trying to change who you are in seven days. You are simply throwing the first stone in the right direction.

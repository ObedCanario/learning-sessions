Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter two: what choice is.

Here is a claim that should stop you for a moment. Aristotle says your choices reveal your character more accurately than your actions do. [pause 1.5]

Why would that be true? Two people can perform exactly the same action for completely different reasons, and only one of them actually decided to do it. The action is what everyone can see, but the choice behind it is where the person really lives.

So here is what to listen for. Aristotle is going to define choice by elimination, the way a detective eliminates suspects. Choice is not desire, it is not anger, it is not wishing, and it is not belief. Watch each suspect get crossed off the list, and pay attention to what remains standing at the end.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-3}}

Let's anchor that distinction carefully before we go any further, because everything else in the chapter depends on it: every choice is willing, but not every willing act is a choice.

Think of a circle labeled "willing," with a smaller circle inside it labeled "chosen." When a child grabs a cookie, the grab is willing, because nobody forced the child's hand. But the child did not deliberate, weigh options, and decide. The same is true when you snap at someone in the heat of the moment. It was yours, but it was not chosen. Choice is the smaller, more deliberate circle in the middle. [pause 2]

{{read 4-5}}

The first suspect is desire, and the strongest evidence against it is the person with strong willpower.

Picture someone who is committed to a healthier diet, standing in front of a bakery window on the way home from work. Desire says, "Go inside," while choice says, "Keep walking." If choice were simply another desire, that would be one desire against another, and the louder one would win every time. But that is not what happens in the mind of a disciplined person. Something different, something higher, overrules the desire. That something is choice.

Here is the first principle. Desire pulls you toward what feels good right now, while choice steers you toward what you have judged to be good. Desire is the engine, and choice is the steering wheel. [pause 2]

{{read 6-7}}

Now we reach what may be the most practical distinction in the entire chapter, the difference between wishing for something and actually choosing it.

Aristotle says wishing is about the destination, while choosing is about the route. You wish to be healthy, but you cannot simply choose health. What you can choose is the walk this morning, the vegetables at dinner, and going to bed an hour earlier.

That is why "I choose to be happy" sounds a little strange to him. Happiness is a destination. You choose the steps, and happiness is where those steps eventually lead.

This principle is worth writing down and returning to regularly. Wishes are free, but choices cost you something. Anyone can wish to be wealthy, wise, or fit. Only the person who chooses the daily steps actually arrives. [pause 2]

{{read 8-9}}

The final suspect is belief, and the key distinction is beautifully simple. Beliefs are judged as true or false, while choices are judged as good or bad.

You can hold an excellent belief and still make a terrible choice. Plenty of people believe that exercise is healthy, that saving money is wise, and that honesty matters. Their beliefs are correct, but their choices tell a different story. As Aristotle puts it, some people have reasonably good beliefs, but because of bad character, they choose wrongly.

Here is the root of it, stated as simply as possible: knowing is not the same as doing, and character lives in the distance between the two. Your beliefs describe the world, but your choices shape you. [pause 2]

{{read 10}}

So let's put the chapter together.

Choice is not desire, because desire chases pleasure, while choice can overrule it. Choice is not anger, because anger is the least deliberate thing we do. [pause 1.5]

Choice is not wishing, because wishing aims at destinations, while choice works on the route. And choice is not belief, because belief aims at truth, while choice aims at action. [pause 2]

What remains is this: a choice is what you decide after thinking it through. Notice that the very word, in Greek, means picking one option ahead of the others. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Pick one wish you have been carrying around, something like "I wish I were healthier" or "I wish I read more." Then translate it into one specific choice you can make tomorrow morning. Turn the wish into a step.

In chapter three, Aristotle examines the thinking that comes before a choice, the process he calls deliberation. You'll learn why we only deliberate about what we can actually do, and why the first step is the last thing you figure out.

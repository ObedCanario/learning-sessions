Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter eleven: the temperate person and the self-indulgent person.

Here's the question for today's chapter: what does a genuinely self-controlled person actually experience? Are they constantly fighting temptation, gritting their teeth through every meal? [pause 1.5]

Aristotle's answer is surprising. The temperate person isn't someone who desperately wants and heroically resists. It's someone whose wants have been shaped so that they don't suffer much when pleasure is missing.

So here is what to listen for. First, the difference between natural desires and learned ones. Second, the strange idea that the self-indulgent person suffers for the sake of pleasure. Third, what the temperate person enjoys, and what they don't.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-3}}

Let's take that distinction down to its roots.

Some desires are natural. Everybody gets hungry and thirsty. With those, Aristotle says, people go wrong in only one way: too much. Natural hunger is simply about filling an empty stomach. Eating far past that point is going beyond what nature asks for.

Other desires are personal and learned: the specific foods you love, the particular comforts you reach for. These are shaped by your history and your habits. And since they were learned, they can be reshaped.

That's an encouraging point. Many of your cravings weren't installed at birth. They were trained, which means they can be retrained. [pause 2]

{{read 4-7}}

This is the heart of the chapter, so let's slow down and examine Aristotle's reasoning carefully.

Notice how Aristotle defines the self-indulgent person. It isn't simply that they enjoy pleasure. It's that they are more upset than they should be when they can't have it. Even their pain, he says, comes from pleasure.

Then comes one of the most perceptive lines in the book: craving itself hurts. The self-indulgent person suffers twice, once while craving the pleasure, and again when they can't get it. Aristotle calls this ridiculous, suffering pain for the sake of pleasure.

Let's make it modern. Think about checking your phone. The pleasure of a new notification lasts a second. But the restlessness of waiting for one, the itch to check, and the irritation when nothing is there can fill an entire afternoon. You end up feeling worse for the sake of feeling good.

Here is the first principle. Every craving you feed grows stronger, and every craving you starve grows weaker. The self-indulgent person isn't enjoying life more. They are hurting more, because their wants have become demands. [pause 2]

So here is a line worth writing down and remembering: the more desperately you need a pleasure, the less you actually enjoy it. [pause 2]

{{read 8-10}}

Two final observations deserve your attention before we conclude.

First, Aristotle says that people who enjoy too little hardly exist. Temperance is not about being numb, cold, or joyless. A person who enjoyed nothing at all would be, in his words, something other than human.

Second, look at his description of the temperate person. They enjoy pleasant things in moderation, as long as those things don't damage their health, don't conflict with what's right, and don't cost more than they can afford. That's a remarkably practical test, and you can apply it to almost anything.

So let's put the chapter together.

Natural desires go wrong in only one way, which is too much. Learned desires go wrong in many ways. [pause 1.5]

The self-indulgent person suffers twice, while craving and while going without. [pause 1.5]

The temperate person enjoys life, but isn't distressed when a pleasure is missing. Their wants and their reason point in the same direction. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Choose one small pleasure you reach for automatically, and skip it once a day. Notice the craving, and simply watch it. You'll find that it rises, peaks, and fades. Each time you watch it fade, it loses a little of its power over you.

In chapter twelve, the final chapter of Book Three, Aristotle compares self-indulgence with cowardice and shows why our desires need training, the way a child needs a good teacher.

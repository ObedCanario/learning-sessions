Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Book Three. Chapter four: what we wish for.

This is a short chapter, but it carries one of the most useful ideas in the entire book. Here is the question. When you want something, do you want what is actually good, or only what appears good to you? [pause 1.5]

So here is what to listen for. Aristotle will show you that both obvious answers lead to trouble. Then he will offer a third answer built around a measuring stick, and he'll close by naming the most common reason people go wrong.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-4}}

Let's untangle that puzzle carefully, because it is considerably cleverer than it first sounds.

If we only ever wish for what is truly good, then the person who wished for something harmful didn't really wish for it, which is plainly absurd. People wish for harmful things every day.

But if we simply wish for whatever seems good, then there is no real standard at all. One person's medicine is another person's poison, and nobody is ever wrong. That is equally absurd.

So Aristotle needs an answer that keeps a real standard without pretending that everyone already meets it.

{{read 5-6}}

Here is his solution, and the metaphor is worth unpacking carefully.

Think about taste and health. When you have a bad cold, food tastes strange to you. Sweet things might taste bitter, and your favorite meal might seem bland. Does that mean the food changed? No, it means your instrument for judging it is temporarily off.

Aristotle says character works the same way. A person in good moral health perceives things accurately. What is truly good actually appears good to them, and what is truly shameful actually appears shameful. A person with damaged character is like a person with a fever: the world seems different to them, and they trust that distorted impression.

Then comes his central image: the good person is like a ruler or measuring stick. When a carpenter wants to know whether a board is the right length, they don't argue about it. They hold it up against a reliable ruler. When you want to know what is genuinely good, Aristotle suggests a similar method. Ask what a person of excellent character would see in this situation. [pause 2]

Here is the first principle underneath it. What seems good to you depends on the condition of the person doing the seeing. So the deepest way to improve your judgment is not to win more arguments. It is to become a better instrument. [pause 2]

{{read 7}}

There is the diagnosis, in three short sentences. Pleasure looks good even when it isn't.

Let's make it concrete. Scrolling your phone at midnight feels good, so it looks like a good idea. The difficult conversation you keep postponing feels painful, so it looks like a bad idea. Pleasure and pain function like a pair of tinted glasses, coloring everything you look at, and the tint is strongest exactly when the decision matters most.

So let's put the chapter together.

We wish, in the true sense, for what is good. But each of us actually pursues what seems good to us. [pause 1.5]

A good person sees accurately, like a healthy tongue tastes accurately, and that makes them a measuring stick for the rest of us. [pause 1.5]

And the main thing that distorts our vision is pleasure, which can make the wrong thing look right. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Whenever something feels very appealing, ask one extra question: does this look good because it is good, or because it is pleasant? And whenever something feels painful, ask the reverse. Is it bad, or simply uncomfortable?

In chapter five, Aristotle confronts the biggest question of the book: are we responsible for our own character? His answer is direct, demanding, and ultimately very hopeful.

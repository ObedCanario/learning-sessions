Aristotle. Physics, Book Two. Chapter three: the four causes.

Here's the question for today: what does it actually mean to understand something? Not simply to recognize it, or to have heard of it, but to genuinely understand it. [pause 1.5]

Aristotle's answer is simple and demanding at the same time. You understand something when you can explain why it is the way it is. And he argues that the word "why" actually carries four different meanings, which means there are four different answers you need before your understanding is complete.

This short chapter contains one of the most practical intellectual tools Aristotle ever developed. It also prepares the ground for the Ethics, because the fourth cause, the purpose behind a thing, is the foundation on which his entire account of the good life is constructed.

So here is what to listen for. First, the four kinds of cause, together with the ordinary examples he uses to illustrate each one. Second, the observation that a single thing can have several causes at once, and that these causes don't compete with one another. Third, his advice at the very end: whenever you search for a cause, look for the most precise one available.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1}}

Let's take that opening down to the studs.

Aristotle says that people don't believe they know something until they've grasped the reason behind it. Consider how accurate that is. You can memorize that a recipe requires baking soda, but you don't really understand cooking until you know what the baking soda is doing in the mixture. Once you understand the reason, you can adapt, substitute ingredients, and correct your own mistakes, whereas without it you can only follow instructions.

So here is the root of it. Facts describe what is happening, while causes explain why it is happening, and that explanation is exactly what allows you to handle a situation you've never encountered before. [pause 2]

Now he presents the four kinds of cause, one at a time.

{{read 2}}

The first cause is the material, meaning whatever a thing is made of. The statue is made of bronze, and the bowl is made of silver. Notice that he includes the broader categories as well: bronze is a kind of metal, so metal is also part of the answer.

This is the explanation most of us reach for first. If you ask someone what a house is, they might answer wood, brick, and nails. That answer is accurate, but it's incomplete, because a disorganized pile of lumber and bricks is certainly not a house.

{{read 3}}

That brings us to the second cause, which is the form. This is the most difficult of the four, so let's unpack his example carefully.

An octave in music consists of two notes, where one note vibrates exactly twice as fast as the other. That precise relationship, two to one, is what makes it an octave. You can play it on a piano, on a guitar, or with your own voice, and although the material changes completely, the octave remains identical, because the underlying pattern remains identical.

So the form is the pattern, the design, the definition of what the thing essentially is. A house is not merely wood and brick; it's wood and brick arranged in order to shelter people. If you change the material but preserve the design, you still have a house. If you keep the material but destroy the design, you're left with a pile of rubble.

Here's a line worth remembering: material is what a thing is made of, but form is what makes it that particular thing. [pause 2]

{{read 4}}

The third cause is the one we usually intend today whenever we use the word: the origin of the change. Later philosophers named it the efficient cause. It's the maker, the mover, whatever actually sets things in motion.

Look carefully at his examples. The father is a cause of the child, and the sculptor is a cause of the statue. And, interestingly, the person who gave advice is a cause of whatever was eventually done. That deserves a moment of reflection. A single piece of excellent advice, delivered at the right moment, can become the origin of an entire sequence of events in someone's life. Words can be causes too.

{{read 5}}

Now we arrive at the most important cause, the one positioned at the heart of everything Aristotle teaches about living well. It's the end, the goal, "that for the sake of which." Later philosophers called it the final cause.

His example is perfectly ordinary. Why is that man walking around? He's walking in order to be healthy, and once we've given that answer, we feel that we've genuinely explained his behavior.

Let's take it down to the studs. Health doesn't yet exist when he begins walking; it lies somewhere in the future. And nevertheless, it's the reason for everything he's doing at this moment. The goal reaches back from the future and organizes the present.

Then he adds something important about the intermediate steps. Losing weight, medicine, and even surgical instruments all exist for the sake of health. They are means, and although some are activities and others are instruments, every one of them points toward the same destination.

Let's bring this into modern life. Imagine you're building a business. The money, the employees, and the software represent its material. The strategy, and the way everything is organized, represents its form. Your daily effort is the origin of the change. But the purpose, the specific problem you're solving for other people, is the reason all of the other components exist in the first place. If you lose sight of that purpose, the other three causes have nothing to aim at.

So write this down: the purpose arrives last in time, but it comes first in importance. [pause 2]

{{read 6-7}}

Here Aristotle makes an observation that eliminates a tremendous amount of confusion. Because the word "cause" has several meanings, a single thing can have several causes simultaneously, and those causes don't compete. The bronze and the sculptor are both genuine causes of the statue, simply in different ways. When people argue about "the" cause of something, they're frequently providing different kinds of answers to entirely different kinds of questions.

Next come two clever examples. First, hard work causes fitness, and fitness causes hard work. That sounds circular, but it isn't. Hard work produces fitness, which makes it the origin of the change, and fitness is the reason you undertake the hard work initially, which makes it the purpose. Each one causes the other, but in a different sense.

Second, consider the ship and its pilot. When the pilot is aboard, we credit the pilot for the ship's safety, and when the pilot is missing and the ship is wrecked, we blame the absence. So the identical thing can produce opposite results, depending simply on whether it's present or absent.

Think about that in relation to your own life. The habit you maintain is a cause, and the habit you neglect is a cause as well. Many of the difficulties people experience come not from what they did, but from what was missing: the conversation that never happened, the exercise that was skipped, the savings that were never set aside. [pause 2]

{{read 8-9}}

In this passage, Aristotle is organizing everything he's said. Every example so far, he explains, belongs under the same four headings. Letters are the material of syllables, parts are the material of a whole, and even the premises of an argument are the material of its conclusion.

Notice one small but significant remark at the end. The purpose is whatever is best, or at least whatever appears best to the person acting, and he says it makes no difference whether it's the real good or merely the apparent good. A mistaken goal is still a goal, and it still directs behavior. That's precisely why the Ethics devotes so much attention to learning to aim at the right things. A person who aims at the wrong target can still work extremely hard; they simply arrive somewhere they never intended to be.

{{read 10}}

At this point Aristotle becomes more technical, so let's make his reasoning concrete.

Polyclitus was a celebrated sculptor in ancient Greece. If you say "Polyclitus made this statue," you're correct, but in a sense only accidentally. He didn't create the statue because he was Polyclitus; he created it because he was a sculptor, and being Polyclitus just happens to coincide with being a sculptor. And if you say "a pale man made it" or "a musical man made it," you've wandered even further away from the real cause.

Here's the root of it. Not everything that's true about a cause actually explains the effect. The sculptor produced the statue because of his skill, not because of his name, his complexion, or his musical preferences.

{{read 11-13}}

This is the most abstract section of the chapter, so let's extract the one idea you most need.

Aristotle distinguishes a cause that is capable of acting from a cause that is acting right now. A house builder remains a cause of houses even while he's asleep, and that's the potential cause. But a house builder who is actively building is the actual cause, and that kind of cause begins and ends together with its effect. When the building stops, the being built stops too.

Consider your own abilities. Knowing how to do something and actually doing it are two very different conditions. A skill you possess but never use produces nothing. Potential is real, but it only influences the world when it becomes action. [pause 2]

{{read 14-16}}

And here's the practical advice that concludes the chapter: always look for the most precise cause.

Why does a man build? Because he's a builder. But why is a builder able to build? Because he possesses the art, the skill, of building. That skill is the deeper and more precise cause, so it's the one to concentrate on.

Apply this principle to any result you want to understand. Why did a particular company succeed? Saying "because of its founder" is true, but vague. The precise cause is the specific skill or decision that actually made the difference. And if you'd like to produce the same result in your own life, the precise cause is the one you can actually learn and repeat.

So let's put the chapter together.

To know something is to know the reason behind it. [pause 1.5]

There are four kinds of explanation: what a thing is made of, what pattern makes it what it is, what set it in motion, and what it exists for. [pause 1.5]

A single thing can have several causes at once, and an absence can be a cause, just like a presence. [pause 1.5]

An unused skill produces nothing. And whenever you search for a cause, look for the most precise one. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Choose one thing in your life that matters to you, whether it's your work, a project, or a habit, and write down its four causes. What is it made of? What is its design? Who or what keeps it moving? And most importantly, what is it for? If that final answer is unclear, that's exactly where your attention should go first.

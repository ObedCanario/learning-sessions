Aristotle. Physics, Book Two. Chapter three: the four causes.

Here's the question for today: what does it actually mean to understand something? Not simply to recognize it, or to have heard of it, but to genuinely understand it. [pause 1.5]

Aristotle's answer is simple and demanding at the same time. You understand something when you can explain why it is the way it is. And he argues that the word "why" actually carries four different meanings, which means there are four different answers you need before your understanding is complete.

This short chapter contains one of the most practical intellectual tools Aristotle ever developed. It also prepares the ground for the Ethics, because the fourth cause, the purpose behind a thing, is the foundation on which his entire account of the good life is constructed.

So here is what to listen for. First, the four kinds of cause, together with the ordinary examples he uses to illustrate each one. Second, the observation that a single thing can have several causes at once, and that these causes don't compete with one another. Third, his advice at the very end: whenever you search for a cause, look for the most exact one available.

One more note before we begin. Today's reading is a plain-English version of the chapter. Whenever you hear my voice inside the reading, that's a short explanation that has been added, not Aristotle's own words.

Let's hear it from Aristotle.

{{read 1-4}}

Let's take that opening down to the studs.

Aristotle says that people don't believe they know something until they've grasped the reason behind it. Consider how accurate that is. You can memorize that a recipe requires baking soda, but you don't really understand cooking until you know what the baking soda is doing in the mixture. Once you understand the reason, you can adapt, substitute ingredients, and correct your own mistakes, whereas without it you can only follow instructions.

So here is the root of it. Facts describe what is happening, while causes explain why it is happening, and that explanation is exactly what allows you to handle a situation you've never encountered before. [pause 2]

{{read 5-7}}

The material cause is the explanation most of us reach for first. If you ask someone what a house is, they might answer wood, brick, and nails. That answer is accurate, but it's incomplete, because a disorganized pile of lumber and bricks is certainly not a house.

{{read 8}}

The formal cause is the most difficult of the four, so let's unpack the octave carefully.

You can play an octave on a piano, on a guitar, or with your own voice, and although the material changes completely, the octave remains identical, because the underlying pattern remains identical. So the form is the pattern, the design, the definition of what the thing essentially is.

Now apply it to the house. A house is not merely wood and brick; it's wood and brick arranged in order to shelter people. If you change the material but preserve the design, you still have a house. If you keep the material but destroy the design, you're left with a pile of rubble.

Here's a line worth remembering: material is what a thing is made of, but form is what makes it that particular thing. [pause 2]

{{read 9}}

The efficient cause is the one we usually intend today whenever we use the word "cause." It's the maker, the mover, whatever actually sets things in motion.

And notice that Aristotle includes the person who gives advice. That deserves a moment of reflection. A single piece of excellent advice, delivered at the right moment, can become the origin of an entire sequence of events in someone's life. Words can be causes too.

{{read 10-12}}

Now we arrive at the most important cause, the one positioned at the heart of everything Aristotle teaches about living well.

Let's take it down to the studs. Health doesn't yet exist when the man begins walking; it lies somewhere in the future. And nevertheless, it's the reason for everything he's doing at this moment. The goal reaches back from the future and organizes the present. Every step along the way, whether it's an action or a tool, gets its meaning from the destination it points toward.

Let's bring this into modern life. Imagine you're building a business. The money, the employees, and the software represent its material. The strategy, and the way everything is organized, represents its form. Your daily effort is the origin of the change. But the purpose, the specific problem you're solving for other people, is the reason all of the other components exist in the first place. If you lose sight of that purpose, the other three causes have nothing to aim at.

So write this down: the purpose arrives last in time, but it comes first in importance. [pause 2]

{{read 13-16}}

Here Aristotle makes an observation that eliminates a tremendous amount of confusion. A single thing can have several causes simultaneously, and those causes don't compete. When people argue about "the" cause of something, they're frequently providing different kinds of answers to entirely different kinds of questions.

Consider the hard work and the fitness. It sounds circular, but it isn't. Hard work produces fitness, which makes it the origin of the change, and fitness is the reason you undertake the hard work initially, which makes it the purpose. Each one causes the other, but in a different sense.

And think about the pilot in relation to your own life. The habit you maintain is a cause, and the habit you neglect is a cause as well. Many of the difficulties people experience come not from what they did, but from what was missing: the conversation that never happened, the exercise that was skipped, the savings that were never set aside. [pause 2]

{{read 17-24}}

In this passage, Aristotle is organizing everything he's said under the same four headings, so let's focus on one small but significant remark at the end.

The purpose is whatever is best, or at least whatever appears best to the person acting, and it makes no difference whether it's the real good or merely the apparent good. A mistaken goal is still a goal, and it still directs behavior. That's precisely why the Ethics devotes so much attention to learning to aim at the right things. A person who aims at the wrong target can still work extremely hard; they simply arrive somewhere they never intended to be. [pause 2]

{{read 25-31}}

At this point Aristotle becomes more technical, so let's extract the principle underneath.

Polyclitus did make the statue, but he didn't make it because he was Polyclitus; he made it because he was a sculptor. His name, his complexion, and his musical preferences all happen to be true of him, but none of them explains the statue.

Here's the root of it. Not everything that's true about a cause actually explains the effect. When you study someone's success, it's easy to copy the details that merely came along with it, such as their morning routine, their clothes, or their favorite books, instead of the skill that actually produced the result. [pause 2]

{{read 32-40}}

You don't need to memorize the six ways of describing a cause. The one idea from this stretch worth carrying with you is the difference between a cause that is able to act and a cause that is acting right now.

A house builder remains a cause of houses even while asleep, but only as a possibility. The builder who is actively building is the actual cause, and that kind of cause begins and ends together with its effect. When the building stops, the being built stops too.

Consider your own abilities. Knowing how to do something and actually doing it are two very different conditions. A skill you possess but never use produces nothing. Potential is real, but it only influences the world when it becomes action. [pause 2]

{{read 41-47}}

And here's the practical advice that concludes the chapter: always look for the most exact cause. The builder builds because of the skill of building, so the skill is the deeper cause, and it's the one to concentrate on.

Apply this principle to any result you want to understand. Why did a particular company succeed? Saying "because of its founder" is true, but vague. The exact cause is the specific skill or decision that actually made the difference. And if you'd like to produce the same result in your own life, the exact cause is the one you can actually learn and repeat.

So let's put the chapter together.

To know something is to know the reason behind it. [pause 1.5]

There are four kinds of explanation: what a thing is made of, what pattern makes it what it is, what set it in motion, and what it exists for. [pause 1.5]

A single thing can have several causes at once, and an absence can be a cause, just like a presence. [pause 1.5]

An unused skill produces nothing. And whenever you search for a cause, look for the most exact one. [pause 2]

Here is a practice for this week. Choose one thing in your life that matters to you, whether it's your work, a project, or a habit, and write down its four causes. What is it made of? What is its design? Who or what keeps it moving? And most importantly, what is it for? If that final answer is unclear, that's exactly where your attention should go first.

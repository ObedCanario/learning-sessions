Chapter 1: Willing and unwilling actions

The main point: an action is unwilling only if it is forced on you from outside, or if you didn't know key facts about what you were doing. Everything else you do is willing, including things done from anger or desire.

Why does this matter? Virtue is about how we feel and how we act. When someone acts willingly, we praise or blame them. When someone acts unwillingly, we forgive them, and sometimes we feel sorry for them. So if you want to understand virtue, you need to know the difference between willing and unwilling acts. Lawmakers need to know it too, so they can decide whom to reward and whom to punish.

An act is unwilling in two cases. One, it was forced on you. Two, you did it because you didn't know something.

What counts as forced? An act is forced when the cause comes from outside you, and you add nothing to it. Picture a strong wind blowing you somewhere. Or picture men who have captured you dragging you somewhere. You didn't move yourself. Something else moved you.

Now comes a harder case. Sometimes you do something out of fear of something worse, or to achieve something good. Imagine a cruel ruler has your parents and children. He orders you to do something shameful. If you do it, they live. If you refuse, they die. Is that act willing or unwilling? People disagree.

Here is a similar case. A ship is caught in a storm. To keep it from sinking, the sailors throw the cargo overboard. Normally, no one would want to throw away their goods. But to save their own lives and the crew's, any sensible person would do it.

Acts like these are mixed. But they are closer to willing acts. Here is why. At the moment you do them, they really are the best choice. And whether an act is a good idea depends on the situation you're in. So when we ask "was this willing?", we have to ask it about that moment. In that moment, the person does act willingly. They move their own arms and legs. The cause is inside them, and when the cause is inside you, it is up to you whether to act. So these acts are willing. But in general, they are perhaps unwilling, since no one would pick such an act for its own sake.

People are sometimes even praised for acts like these. This happens when someone puts up with something shameful or painful to gain something great and good. If they do the opposite, they get blamed. Suffering terrible shame for no good reason, or for a small reason, is the sign of a weak person.

Some acts don't earn praise, but they do earn forgiveness. This happens when someone does wrong under pressure that is too much for any human, pressure that no one could stand up to.

But there may be some acts that nothing could force us to do. We should die after the worst suffering rather than do them. Take Alcmaeon in the play by Euripides. [In that story, Alcmaeon kills his own mother. He says he had to, because his father made him swear to do it and threatened him with curses.] His reasons for being "forced" seem ridiculous.

It is sometimes hard to judge what we should choose, and what price is worth paying. It is hard to judge what we should put up with, and for what gain. And it is even harder to stick to our decision once we make it. Usually, what we expect to suffer is painful, and what we are pushed to do is shameful. That is why we praise or blame people depending on whether they gave in to the pressure or not.

So, which acts should we call forced? Strictly speaking, acts are forced when the cause is completely outside, and the person adds nothing. Then there are acts that are unwanted in themselves, but worth choosing right now, for a certain gain, and whose cause is inside the person. Those acts are unwanted in general, but willing right now, for that gain. They are more like willing acts. That's because actions always happen in specific situations, and in this specific situation, the act is willing. It's not easy to give a rule for what's worth choosing in exchange for what. Every case is different.

Some people might say: "Pleasure and noble goals pull on us from outside. So they force us." But if that were true, every act would be forced. Pleasure and noble goals are the reasons for everything anyone does. Also, people who are forced to act feel pain. People who act for pleasure or for something noble feel pleasure. And it's silly to blame outside things instead of yourself for being easily tempted. It's also silly to take credit for your good acts, but blame pleasure for your bad ones. So a forced act really is one where the cause is outside and the person adds nothing.

Now let's look at acts done because of not knowing.

Anything done because you didn't know something is "not willing." But it only counts as truly "unwilling" if you feel sorry and regret it afterward. Think of someone who did something by mistake and doesn't care at all. They didn't act willingly, because they didn't know what they were doing. But they didn't act unwillingly either, because they aren't upset. So we split these people in two groups. The one who regrets it acted unwillingly. The one who doesn't regret it is different, so we give him a different label: he acted "non-willingly." Since he is different, he deserves his own name.

There's another difference. Acting because you don't know something is not the same as acting while not knowing. Think of someone drunk or in a rage. They don't act because of missing facts. They act because of the drink or the anger. But they still act without knowing what they are doing.

Every bad person is ignorant of what they should do and what they should avoid. This kind of mistake is what makes people unfair and bad. But we don't call an act unwilling just because someone didn't know what was good for them. Having bad goals doesn't make an act unwilling. It makes it wicked. And not knowing the general rules of right and wrong doesn't excuse you either. People get blamed for that. What excuses you is not knowing the specific facts of the situation: the details of what you're doing and who or what it affects. Pity and forgiveness depend on these details. If you didn't know one of them, you acted unwillingly.

So let's list these details. There are six. A person might not know:
who is acting;
what they are doing;
who or what they are acting on;
what they are using, such as which tool;
what the act will lead to; for example, they think it will keep someone safe;
how they are doing it, such as gently or roughly.

No one could be ignorant of all six unless they were insane. And no one could be ignorant of who is acting, because how could you not know yourself? But you could easily not know what you are doing. Here are some examples. People say, "It slipped out while I was talking." Or, "I didn't know it was a secret." [The playwright Aeschylus said this when he was accused of revealing the secrets of a religious ceremony.] Or someone says, "I only meant to show how the catapult works, and it went off by accident."

Here are more examples. You might think your own son is an enemy, as Merope does in an old story. [She almost kills her son because she doesn't recognize him.] You might think a spear has a safety cap on its point, like a practice spear, when it really has a sharp tip. You might think a stone is pumice, a light, harmless stone, when it is really a heavy one. You might give someone a medicine to save them, and it kills them. Or you might mean to just tap someone, like in a friendly boxing match, and actually hurt them.

The ignorance can be about any of these details. Someone who didn't know one of them is thought to have acted unwillingly. This is especially true if they didn't know the most important ones, which are what they were doing and what it would lead to. But again, for the act to count as unwilling, the person must feel pain and regret about it afterward.

So, unwilling acts are forced, or done out of ignorance. That means a willing act is one where the cause is inside the person, and the person knows the specific facts of what they're doing.

What about acts done out of anger or desire? They should probably not be called unwilling. Here's why.
First, if they were unwilling, then no animal and no child would ever act willingly.
Second, do we really mean that nothing we do out of desire or anger is willing? Or that our good acts are willing and our bad ones are not? That's silly, since the same cause leads to both.
Third, it would be strange to call it unwilling when we desire the things we should desire. And we should get angry at some things, and we should desire some things, like health and learning.
Fourth, unwilling acts are painful. But acting on desire feels pleasant.
Fifth, mistakes made in anger and mistakes made after careful thinking are both to be avoided. Why call one willing and the other not?
Finally, feelings are as human as reason is. So acts that come from anger or desire are still the person's own acts. It would be strange to call them unwilling.

Chapter 2: What choice is

The main point: choosing is not the same as wanting, desiring, getting angry, or believing. It is something we decide after thinking it through.

Now that we've sorted out willing and unwilling acts, let's look at choice. Choice seems closely tied to virtue. In fact, the choices people make show their character better than their actions do.

Every choice is willing, but not every willing act is a choice. Willing acts include more. Children and animals act willingly, but they don't make choices. And when we do something on the spur of the moment, we call it willing, but not chosen.

Some people say choice is desire, or anger, or wishing, or a kind of belief. They're wrong. Let's take each one.

Choice is not desire. Here's why.
Animals have desires, but they don't make choices.
A person with weak willpower acts on desire, not choice. A person with strong willpower does the opposite: they act on choice, against their desire.
Choice can fight against desire, but one desire doesn't fight another in the same way.
Desire is about what feels good or bad. Choice is not.

Choice is even less like anger. Things done in anger seem the least chosen of all.

Choice is not wishing, though the two are close. Here's why.
You can't choose something impossible. If you said you did, people would think you were foolish. But you can wish for impossible things, like living forever.
You can wish for things you have no control over, like wishing your favorite actor or athlete wins a contest. But you can't choose that. You only choose things you think you can make happen yourself.
Wishing is about the goal. Choosing is about how to get there. For example, we wish to be healthy, but we choose the things that will make us healthy. We wish to be happy, and we say so. But it would sound odd to say "I choose to be happy." In general, choice is about things that are up to us.

For the same reason, choice is not belief. Here's why.
You can have beliefs about anything: things that never change, impossible things, and things that are up to you. Choice is only about things that are up to you.
Beliefs are judged as true or false. Choices are judged as good or bad.

Maybe nobody says choice is the same as belief in general. But choice isn't even the same as one special kind of belief. Here's why.
Our choices make us a certain kind of person. Our beliefs don't.
We choose to get something or avoid something. But we hold beliefs about what something is, who it's good for, or how. You can't "believe" your way into getting or avoiding something.
A choice is praised for aiming at the right thing. A belief is praised for being true.
We choose things we clearly know are good. But we hold beliefs about things we're not quite sure of.
The people who make the best choices aren't always the ones with the best beliefs. Some people have pretty good beliefs, but because of bad character, they choose wrongly.
It doesn't matter whether a belief comes before a choice or along with it. That's not the question. The question is whether choice is a kind of belief. It isn't.

So what is choice? It is willing, but not everything willing is chosen. Maybe choice is what we decide after thinking it over first. At least, choice involves reasoning and thought. Even the word itself suggests picking one option ahead of the others.

Chapter 3: Thinking things through

The main point: we only think carefully about things we can actually do, and we think about how to reach a goal, not about the goal itself.

Do we think carefully about everything? Or are some things not worth thinking over? Let's only count what a sensible person would think over, not what a fool or a madman might.

No one thinks over things that never change. For example, no one deliberates about how the universe is built, or about a fixed fact of math, like the fact that the side and diagonal of a square can't be measured with the same unit.
No one thinks over things that move, but always the same way, whether by necessity or by nature. Examples are the seasons and the rising of the stars.
No one thinks over things that happen one way sometimes and another way other times, like droughts and rain.
No one thinks over pure luck, like finding buried treasure.
We don't even think over all human matters. For example, no one in Sparta sits down to plan the best government for a faraway people like the Scythians.
That's because none of these things can be done by our own efforts.

We think things over when they are up to us and can be done. In fact, that's all that's left. The causes of things are nature, necessity, luck, and human thought and action. Each group of people thinks over what they themselves can do.

In exact fields with fixed rules, there's nothing to think over. Take spelling. We know exactly how letters should be written. But we do think things over when our efforts matter and the result isn't always the same, such as in medicine or making money. We think things over more in sailing a ship than in physical training, because sailing is less exact. The same pattern holds elsewhere. We also think things over more in the practical skills than in the sciences, because we're less certain in the skills. We think things over when something usually happens a certain way, but the result is unclear, or when the outcome is uncertain. And on big questions, we ask others for help, because we don't trust ourselves to decide alone.

We think about how to reach a goal, not about the goal itself. A doctor doesn't ask whether to heal. A speaker doesn't ask whether to persuade. A leader doesn't ask whether to bring order. No one questions their goal. They take the goal for granted. Then they ask how to reach it.

If there are several ways, they ask which is easiest and best. If there's only one way, they ask how that way works, and what they need to make it work. They keep tracing back step by step until they reach the very first step they must take. That first step to take is the last thing they figure out. It's like solving a geometry problem by working backwards. (Not every search for answers is this kind of thinking. Math problems, for example, aren't. But this kind of thinking is always a search.) What comes last in the planning comes first in the doing.

If we hit something impossible, we give up. For example, if we need money and there's no way to get it, we stop. If it seems possible, we try. By "possible," I mean things we can do ourselves. In a way, this includes what our friends can do for us, because we're still the ones who start the action.

Sometimes we think about which tools we need. Sometimes we think about how to use them. In the same way, we sometimes think about what we need, and sometimes about how to use it or how to get it.

So, as we said, a person is the starting point of their own actions. We only think over things we ourselves can do. And actions are done for the sake of something else. So we don't think about the goal, only how to reach it. We also don't think about basic facts we can see for ourselves, like "Is this bread?" or "Is it baked enough?" Those we know by looking. If we had to think over everything, we'd never stop.

What we think over and what we choose are the same thing. The difference is that by the time we choose, it's settled. We choose what we decided on after thinking it over. You stop asking how to act once you trace things back to yourself, to the part of you that's in charge. That's the part that chooses. You can see this in the old kingdoms described by Homer: the kings decided, then announced their choices to the people.

So choice is about things that are up to us, and that we want after thinking them over. In short, choice is a thought-out desire for something within our power. Once we've decided by thinking it over, our desire follows that decision.

That's an outline of choice: what it's about, and that it's about how to reach goals.

Chapter 4: What we wish for

The main point: the truly good person wishes for what is truly good. Others wish for whatever seems good to them, and pleasure often fools them.

We've said that wishing is about goals. Some people say we wish for what is actually good. Others say we wish for whatever seems good to us. Both views run into problems.

Problem with the first view: say someone chooses badly. Then, on this view, what they wished for wasn't really something they wished for. To count, it would have to be good, but in this case it was bad.

Problem with the second view: if we wish for whatever seems good, then there's no natural thing to wish for. It's just whatever seems good to each person. And different things seem good to different people, even opposite things.

If neither result is acceptable, here's a better answer. In the true sense, we wish for what is good. But for each person, it's what seems good to them. A good person wishes for what is truly good. A bad person might wish for anything at all.

It's like food and health. Food that's truly healthy is healthy for a body in good shape. A sick body might need different things. The same is true of what tastes bitter or sweet, what feels hot or heavy, and so on. A good person judges each thing correctly. The truth shows itself to them. Each kind of character has its own idea of what is good and pleasant. What makes the good person stand out most is that they see the truth in each case. They are like a ruler or a measuring stick that others can be measured against.

Most people go wrong because of pleasure. Pleasure looks good even when it isn't. So we choose what is pleasant, thinking it's good, and we avoid pain, thinking it's bad.

Chapter 5: We are responsible for our character

The main point: being good or bad is up to us. We build our character through our repeated actions, so we are responsible for it.

The goal is what we wish for. The ways of getting there are what we think over and choose. So our actions about those ways are chosen and willing. Practicing virtue is about those ways. So virtue is up to us, and so is vice.

Here's why. If it's up to us to act, it's also up to us not to act, and the other way around. So if doing the right thing is up to us, then failing to do it, which is wrong, is also up to us. And if holding back is the right thing, then doing it anyway, which is wrong, is also up to us. Doing good or bad things is up to us, and so is not doing them. And that's what being a good or bad person means. So whether we are good or bad is up to us.

There's an old saying: "No one is bad on purpose, and no one is happy by accident." It's half right. It's true that no one is happy by accident. But being bad is something we do on purpose. Otherwise we'd have to deny what we just said, and deny that a person is the source of their own actions, the way a parent is the source of a child. But if that's clearly true, and we can't trace our actions to any source outside us, then actions that start in us are up to us and willing.

Both ordinary people and lawmakers act as if this is true. They punish people who do bad things, unless the person was forced, or didn't know something and wasn't to blame for not knowing it. They honor people who do good things. They do this to encourage the good and discourage the bad. But no one tries to encourage us to do things that aren't up to us. There's no point in telling someone not to feel hot, or in pain, or hungry. They'll feel it anyway.

We even punish people for not knowing, if it's their fault that they didn't know. For example, some laws give double punishment to someone who commits a crime while drunk. The cause was in them, because they could have chosen not to get drunk. Getting drunk is what made them not know what they were doing. We also punish people who don't know a law they should know, if it isn't hard to learn. The same goes for anything people don't know because they were careless. We assume they could have known, since they could have paid attention.

But maybe someone is just the careless type. Even so, they made themselves that type by living carelessly. People make themselves unfair by cheating, and make themselves self-indulgent by spending their time drinking and partying. Doing something over and over is what shapes your character. You can see this with people training for a sport or a contest. They practice the same activity all the time. Only a completely senseless person wouldn't know that our character comes from what we repeatedly do.

Also, it makes no sense to say that someone who keeps acting unfairly doesn't want to be unfair. Or that someone who keeps overindulging doesn't want to be self-indulgent. If someone knowingly does things that make them unfair, they become unfair willingly.

But that doesn't mean they can just wish to stop being unfair and become fair. A sick person can't become healthy just by wishing it, either. Imagine someone who got sick through their own fault, by living without self-control and ignoring their doctors. At the start, they could have avoided getting sick. But now it's too late. They threw away their chance. It's like throwing a stone. Once it leaves your hand, you can't get it back. But throwing it was up to you, because you started the motion. In the same way, an unfair or self-indulgent person could have avoided becoming that way at the start. So they became that way willingly. But now that they are that way, they can't simply stop.

This doesn't only apply to faults of character. Some bodily faults are also our own doing, and we blame people for them. No one blames a person who was born ugly. But we do blame someone who looks bad because they never exercise or take care of themselves. The same goes for weakness and disability. No one would scold a person born blind, or blinded by illness or an injury. We'd feel sorry for them. But everyone would blame someone who went blind from heavy drinking or some other excess. So we blame the bodily faults that are our own doing, and not the ones that aren't. If that's true for the body, then the faults of character we blame must also be up to us.

Now someone might object like this: "Everyone goes after what seems good to them. But we don't control what seems good. How the goal looks to each person depends on their character."

Here's the answer. If each person is somehow responsible for their own character, then they are also somehow responsible for how things look to them. If not, then no one is responsible for the bad things they do. Everyone would just be doing bad things out of ignorance, thinking it would get them what's best. Aiming at the right goal wouldn't be something we choose. Instead, we'd have to be born with a kind of inner eye that lets us see what is truly good. Someone born with a good inner eye would be lucky by nature. This would be the greatest and finest gift, something you can't get or learn from anyone. You'd just have it the way you were born with it. Having it would be perfect natural excellence.

But if that were true, how would virtue be any more willing than vice? For the good person and the bad person alike, the goal would be set by nature, or however else. And both would do everything they do based on that goal.

So there are two possibilities. Maybe how the goal looks to each person isn't set purely by nature, and partly depends on them. Or maybe the goal is set by nature, but virtue is still willing because the good person chooses the ways to reach it willingly. Either way, vice is just as willing. The bad person, too, controls their own actions, even if not their goal. So if virtues are willing, as people say, vices are willing too. (We are partly responsible for our own character, and the kind of person we are shapes what we see as our goal.) The same logic applies to both.

Let's sum up what we've said about virtue in general. Virtues are a middle point between two extremes. They are settled parts of our character. They lead us to do the same kinds of acts that built them in the first place. They are up to us and willing. And they act as good reasoning tells them to.

But actions and character are not willing in the same way. We control our actions from start to finish, as long as we know the facts. With character, we control how it starts, but we don't notice each small step as it grows. It's like an illness that creeps up slowly. Still, since it was up to us to act one way or another, our character is willing.

Now let's look at each virtue one at a time. We'll say what each one is, what it deals with, and how. Along the way, it will become clear how many there are. Let's start with courage.

Chapter 6: What courage is about

The main point: courage is mostly about facing death, and especially a noble death in battle.

We've already shown that courage is the middle point between too much fear and too much boldness. What we fear are frightening things, which are, simply put, bad things. That's why fear is sometimes defined as expecting something bad.

We fear all bad things: shame, poverty, illness, having no friends, death. But a brave person isn't concerned with all of these.

Some things we should fear. It's right and good to fear them, and wrong not to. Shame is one. A person who fears shame is decent and modest. A person who doesn't is shameless. Some people call the shameless person brave, but that's stretching the word. He only seems like a brave person because both are fearless.

Maybe we shouldn't fear poverty or illness, or in general things that aren't caused by bad character or by our own fault. But even a person who doesn't fear these isn't truly brave. We sometimes call them brave because of a likeness. For example, some people are cowards in war but are calm and generous about losing money.

And a man isn't a coward just because he fears his wife and children being insulted, or fears being envied, or anything like that. Nor is he brave just because he stays calm when he's about to be whipped.

So which frightening things does courage deal with? The biggest ones. No one stands firm against what is truly terrifying better than a brave person. Death is the most frightening thing of all, because it's the end. Once you're dead, nothing seems good or bad for you anymore.

But a brave person isn't concerned with every kind of death, such as drowning at sea or dying of illness. Which kind, then? The most noble kind. That means death in battle, which happens in the greatest and most noble danger. That's why cities and kings honor those who die in battle.

So, properly speaking, a brave person is fearless when facing a noble death, and in any sudden emergency that could bring death. The emergencies of war are the clearest example.

A brave person is also fearless at sea or when sick. But not in the same way that sailors are. The brave person has given up hope of being saved and hates the idea of dying that way. The sailors stay hopeful because of their experience. Also, we show courage where we can fight bravely, or where dying is noble. Drowning or dying of disease offers neither.

Chapter 7: The brave person, the reckless person, and the coward

The main point: the brave person fears the right things in the right way. The reckless person is too bold, and the coward is too fearful.

Not everyone finds the same things frightening. But some things are beyond what any human can handle. Every sensible person fears those. The frightening things that humans can handle differ in size and degree. So do the things that make us feel bold.

A brave person is as fearless as a human can be. So he will feel fear even of things humans can handle. But he will face them the right way, as good reasoning says, for the sake of honor. Honor is the goal of virtue.

But you can fear these things too much or too little. You can also fear things that aren't really frightening, as if they were. Mistakes come in several forms: fearing the wrong thing, fearing in the wrong way, fearing at the wrong time, and so on. The same goes for feeling bold.

So the brave person faces and fears the right things, for the right reason, in the right way, at the right time. He also feels bold under the right conditions. He feels and acts as the situation deserves, and as good reasoning says.

The goal of any activity is to fit the character it comes from. This is true for the brave person too. Courage is noble, so its goal is noble too, since a thing is defined by its goal. So the brave person endures and acts as courage directs, for a noble goal.

Now let's look at the extremes.

Someone who has too much fearlessness doesn't have a name. (We said earlier that many character traits don't have names.) But he'd be some kind of madman, or someone who can't feel anything, if he feared nothing at all, not earthquakes, not giant waves. That's what people say about the Celts. [Ancient stories said the Celts would charge into the sea with weapons to fight the waves.]

Someone who is too bold about things that really are frightening is reckless. The reckless person is often a show-off who only pretends to be brave. He wants to look the way a brave person really is. So he copies the brave person whenever he can. That's why most reckless people are really a mix of recklessness and cowardice. They act bold when it's easy, but when real danger comes, they don't stand firm.

Someone who has too much fear is a coward. He fears the wrong things, in the wrong ways, and all the rest. He also lacks boldness. But he stands out most for how much he fears painful situations. A coward is a hopeless sort of person, because he fears everything. A brave person is the opposite, because boldness is a sign of hope.

So the coward, the reckless person, and the brave person all deal with the same things. They just react differently. The coward and the reckless person go too far one way or the other. The brave person stays in the middle, which is the right place. Reckless people rush in. They're eager for danger beforehand, but they back off once they're in it. Brave people are calm beforehand, but sharp and ready when it's time to act.

So, as we've said, courage is the middle point between fear and boldness, in the situations we described. It chooses to face things because that is noble, or because not facing them would be shameful.

But killing yourself to escape poverty, heartbreak, or any pain is not brave. It's cowardly. Running away from trouble is weakness. That person faces death not because it is noble, but to escape something bad.

Chapter 8: Five things that look like courage but aren't

The main point: some people look brave, but they're acting from the wrong reasons. Only facing danger because it is noble is true courage.

That's what courage is. But the word is also used for five other things that look like it.

First: the citizen soldier. This is closest to true courage. Citizen soldiers face danger because of legal punishments, because of the shame they'd face otherwise, and because of the honor they'd win. That's why the bravest nations are the ones where cowards are shamed and brave people are honored. Homer describes this kind of courage in his heroes. Hector says to himself, "Polydamas will be the first to blame me." And Diomedes worries that someday Hector will boast to the Trojans, "Diomedes was scared and ran from me."

This kind of courage is the most like true courage, because it comes from a virtue. It comes from a sense of shame, from wanting something noble, namely honor, and from avoiding disgrace, which is ugly.

You could put soldiers who are forced by their commanders in the same group. But they're worse. They act from fear, not shame. They avoid pain, not disgrace. Their commanders force them, as Hector does when he warns: "If I see anyone hiding from the fight, the dogs will eat him." Commanders who beat soldiers for retreating do the same. So do commanders who line soldiers up with ditches behind them, so they can't run. All of these use force. But you should be brave because it's noble, not because you're forced to.

Second: experience. Knowing a lot about a specific kind of danger is also mistaken for courage. That's why Socrates thought courage was a kind of knowledge. People show this in different kinds of danger. Professional soldiers show it in war. War is full of false alarms, and these soldiers have seen them all. So they look brave, because others don't know what's really happening.

Their experience also makes them good at attacking and defending. They know how to use their weapons, and they have the best ones. So they fight like armed men against unarmed men, or like trained athletes against beginners. In those contests too, it's not the bravest who win, but the strongest and fittest.

But professional soldiers turn into cowards when the danger gets too great and they're outnumbered or outgunned. They're the first to run, while citizen soldiers die at their posts. This actually happened in a battle near a temple of Hermes. [There, the hired soldiers fled, but the local citizens stayed and died fighting.] For citizens, running is shameful, and death is better than staying alive that way. But the professionals went in thinking they were stronger. Once they learned they weren't, they ran, because they feared death more than shame. A brave person isn't like that.

Third: rage. Rage is sometimes mistaken for courage. People who act in a rage, like wild animals charging at whoever wounded them, seem brave. That's because brave people also have fire in them. Rage, more than anything, wants to rush into danger. Homer often describes this, with phrases like "he put strength into his rage," "he stirred up their spirit and fury," "he breathed hard," and "his blood boiled." These all describe rage rising up and charging.

But brave people act for honor, and rage only helps them. Wild animals act because they're in pain. They attack because they've been hurt or because they're scared. If they're safe in the forest, they don't come near you. So they aren't brave. Pain and rage push them into danger without seeing any of the risks. If that counted as brave, even a hungry donkey would be brave, since you can't beat it away from its food. And lust also makes people who cheat on their partners do lots of risky things. So creatures pushed into danger by pain or rage aren't brave.

Still, the courage that comes from rage seems the most natural kind. Add real choice and the right reason, and it becomes true courage.

People, like animals, feel pain when they're angry and pleasure when they get revenge. Those who fight for these reasons are fighters, but not brave. They don't act for honor, or as good reasoning says. They act from strong feelings. But they do have something close to courage.

Fourth: overconfidence. Overconfident people aren't brave either. They're bold in danger only because they've won many times against many opponents. They look a lot like brave people, because both are confident. But brave people are confident for the reasons we gave earlier. Overconfident people are confident because they think they're the strongest and nothing can hurt them. (Drunk people act this way too. They get overconfident.) When things don't go their way, they run.

But a brave person faces things that are truly frightening, because doing so is noble and not doing so is shameful. That's why it seems even braver to stay calm in a sudden emergency than in one you saw coming. Staying calm in a surprise must come from character, since there was no time to prepare. When you see something coming, you can plan your response by thinking it through. When it's sudden, only your character can guide you.

Fifth: not knowing the danger. People who don't realize how dangerous something is also look brave. They're close to the overconfident, but worse, because they have no real self-confidence at all. That's why overconfident people at least hold their ground for a while. But people who were simply fooled run away as soon as they learn, or even suspect, that things aren't what they thought. This happened to the Argives in a battle. They thought they were fighting the Sicyonians, a weaker army. [According to the story, the Spartans were carrying Sicyonian shields.] When they realized they were facing the Spartans, they ran.

So we've now described what brave people are like, and what people who only seem brave are like.

Chapter 9: Courage is painful

The main point: courage means facing pain, which is why it deserves praise. And the better your life is, the more painful it is to risk losing it.

Courage deals with both fear and boldness, but not equally. It deals more with fear. Someone who stays steady when facing frightening things, and acts as they should, is braver than someone who handles boldness well. So, as we've said, people are called brave for facing what is painful. That's why courage involves pain, and why it rightly earns praise. It's harder to face pain than to resist pleasure.

Still, the goal of courage seems pleasant. It's just hidden by everything around it. Think of a boxer. His goal is pleasant: the prize and the honor. But the punches hurt his body, and all his effort is painful. There are so many punches and so much effort, and the prize is so small, that the whole thing seems to have nothing pleasant in it.

If courage is like that, then death and wounds will be painful for a brave person, and he won't want them. But he'll face them because it's noble, or because not facing them would be shameful.

And the more virtue someone has, and the happier their life is, the more painful the thought of death will be. Life is most worth living for that kind of person. He knows he's risking losing the best things there are, and that hurts. But he's still brave. Maybe even braver, because he chooses noble acts in war at that cost.

So it's not true that using every virtue feels pleasant. It's only pleasant in the sense that you're reaching the goal.

But here's a twist. The best soldiers may not be people like this. They may be people who are less brave, but who have nothing else good in their lives. Those people are ready to face danger, and they'll trade their lives for small rewards.

That's enough about courage. From what we've said, it's easy to understand its basic nature.

Chapter 10: What temperance is about

The main point: temperance, or self-control, is about the pleasures we share with animals: mainly touch, as in eating, drinking, and sex.

After courage, let's talk about temperance, which means self-control with pleasures. These two seem to be the virtues of the parts of us that aren't reason. We said earlier that temperance is the middle point when it comes to pleasures. (It deals with pain less, and in a different way.) Self-indulgence, which means overdoing pleasures, deals with the same area. So which pleasures are we talking about?

First, let's separate pleasures of the body from pleasures of the mind. Pleasures of the mind include love of honor and love of learning. People who love these enjoy what they love, but their body isn't affected, only their mind. We don't call these people temperate or self-indulgent.

The same goes for other pleasures that aren't of the body. People who love telling and hearing stories, and who spend their days chatting about whatever comes up, we call gossips, not self-indulgent. And people who are upset at losing money or friends aren't called self-indulgent either.

So temperance is about pleasures of the body. But not all of them.

Sight: people who enjoy colors, shapes, and paintings aren't called temperate or self-indulgent. Yet it seems you could enjoy these the right amount, too much, or too little.

Hearing: no one calls people self-indulgent for loving music or theater too much. And no one calls those who enjoy them the right amount temperate.

Smell: we don't use these words for people who enjoy smells, except indirectly. We don't call people self-indulgent for loving the smell of apples, roses, or incense. But we might for people who love the smell of perfumes or rich foods. That's because self-indulgent people enjoy these smells for what they remind them of: the things they crave. Even regular people enjoy the smell of food when they're hungry. But taking great pleasure in that sort of thing is a sign of a self-indulgent person, because to him those are things he craves.

Animals don't get pleasure from these senses, except indirectly. A dog doesn't enjoy the smell of a rabbit. It enjoys eating the rabbit. The smell just told it the rabbit was there. A lion doesn't enjoy the sound of a cow mooing. It enjoys eating the cow. The mooing told it the cow was close, so it seems to enjoy the sound. In the same way, it isn't happy to see a deer or a wild goat. It's happy because it's about to eat.

Temperance and self-indulgence deal with the pleasures that animals share. That's why these pleasures seem low and beastly. They are touch and taste.

But even taste plays a small part. The job of taste is to tell flavors apart, like wine tasters or cooks do when seasoning food. But people don't really enjoy making those distinctions, at least self-indulgent people don't. What they enjoy is the act of indulging itself. And that always happens through touch: in eating, in drinking, and in sex. That's why one glutton wished his throat were longer than a crane's neck. [A crane is a bird with a very long neck.] He wanted to feel the food going down for longer. It was the touch he enjoyed.

So self-indulgence is tied to touch, the sense that all creatures share most widely. That's why self-indulgence is rightly criticized. It belongs to us as animals, not as humans. Enjoying these things, and loving them more than anything else, is beastly.

Even among touch pleasures, the most refined ones are left out. An example is the pleasure of a massage at the gym and the warmth afterward. The touch that a self-indulgent person craves doesn't involve the whole body, only certain parts.

Chapter 11: The temperate person and the self-indulgent person

The main point: the self-indulgent person craves pleasure too much and is upset without it. The temperate person enjoys things in moderation and isn't upset when they're missing.

Some desires are shared by everyone. Others are personal and learned. For example, the desire for food is natural. Everyone who lacks food or drink craves it, sometimes both. And, as Homer says, young and healthy people crave sex. But not everyone wants the same kind of food or the same kind of love. That's why these specific cravings seem personal. Still, they have something natural about them. Different people enjoy different things, and some things are more enjoyable to everyone than random things are.

With natural desires, few people go wrong, and only in one way: too much. Eating or drinking whatever's around until you're stuffed goes beyond what's natural. Natural hunger is just filling an empty stomach. That's why these people are mockingly called "belly-worshippers." They fill their stomachs more than they should. Only people with a very low, slave-like character end up like this.

But with personal pleasures, lots of people go wrong, in lots of ways. When we say someone is "really into" something, we mean they enjoy the wrong things, or enjoy them more than most people do, or enjoy them in the wrong way. Self-indulgent people go wrong in all three ways. They enjoy some things they shouldn't, because those things are disgusting. And when they enjoy things that are fine to enjoy, they enjoy them more than they should, and more than most people do.

So too much pleasure is self-indulgence, and it's a fault.

Pain works differently here than with courage. You aren't called temperate for facing pain, or self-indulgent for avoiding it. Instead, the self-indulgent person is called that because he's more upset than he should be when he can't get pleasant things. (So even his pain comes from pleasure.) And the temperate person is called that because he isn't upset when pleasant things are missing, or when he goes without them.

The self-indulgent person craves all pleasant things, or the most pleasant ones. His cravings lead him to choose them over everything else. So he suffers both when he can't get them and while he's craving them, since craving itself hurts. But it seems ridiculous to suffer pain for the sake of pleasure.

People who enjoy pleasures too little hardly exist. That kind of numbness isn't human. Even animals tell foods apart, liking some and not others. Someone who found nothing pleasant, and liked nothing more than anything else, would be something other than human. There's no name for this person, because they hardly ever exist.

The temperate person is in the middle. He doesn't enjoy what the self-indulgent person loves most; he actually dislikes it. In general, he doesn't enjoy what he shouldn't, and doesn't enjoy anything like this too much. He doesn't feel pain or craving when these things are missing, or only a little, and not more than he should, or at the wrong time, and so on.

When it comes to pleasant things that are good for health and fitness, he wants them in moderation, the right way. He also wants other pleasant things, as long as they don't get in the way of health, aren't against what's right, and don't cost more than he can afford. Someone who ignores these limits loves pleasures more than they're worth. The temperate person isn't like that. He is the kind of person that good reasoning points to.

Chapter 12: Why self-indulgence is more our fault than cowardice

The main point: self-indulgence is more of a choice than cowardice, so it's more blameworthy. Our desires must be trained to listen to reason, the way a child listens to a teacher.

Self-indulgence is more willing than cowardice. Here's why. Self-indulgence is driven by pleasure, and cowardice by pain. We go after pleasure and avoid pain. Pain shakes us up and can throw us off completely. Pleasure doesn't do that. So self-indulgence is more of a choice.

That's also why it's more shameful. It's easy to train yourself to resist pleasures, since life is full of them, and practicing on them is safe. With frightening things, it's the opposite. Practice is dangerous.

But cowardice works in a strange way. Cowardice as a trait is painless. But in the moment, pain shakes us so much that we throw down our weapons and disgrace ourselves in other ways. So our cowardly acts can even seem forced.

With self-indulgence, it's the reverse. Each self-indulgent act is willing, because the person does it out of craving and desire. But the trait as a whole is less willing, because no one wants to be self-indulgent.

The Greek word for self-indulgence literally means "unpunished" or "undisciplined." We also use it for the faults of children, because the two are similar. It doesn't matter here which meaning came first, though clearly the later one is named after the earlier one. And the name fits well. Anything that wants bad things and grows quickly needs to be kept in line. That describes both desire and children best. Children really do live by their desires, and their longing for pleasure is strongest.

If desire doesn't obey reason, it will go to extremes. For a being without reason, the hunger for pleasure is never satisfied, no matter how many sources it tries. Every time you give in to desire, it gets stronger. And if desires grow strong and wild, they can even push out your ability to think clearly.

So desires should be moderate and few, and they should never go against reason. That's what we mean by being obedient and disciplined. Just as a child should follow the directions of their teacher, our desires should follow reason. In a temperate person, desire and reason agree, because both aim at what is good. The temperate person wants the right things, in the right way, at the right time. And that's exactly what reason tells him to do.

That's the end of our discussion of temperance.

Chapter 3: The Four Causes

The main point: to understand anything, you need to know *why* it is the way it is. There are four kinds of cause. Each one can also be described in several ways. A good explanation names the most exact cause.

**Why we study causes**

We have now sorted out some basic points about nature. Next we need to look at causes: what they are like, and how many kinds there are.

Here is why. The goal of our study is knowledge. And people don't think they know a thing until they understand *why* it is so. That means grasping its first, most basic cause. So we must do the same for things that come into being, things that pass away, and every kind of natural change. Once we know their basic principles, we can try to trace each of our questions back to them.

**The four kinds of cause**

The word "cause" is used in four ways.

**First: the material cause.** This is what a thing comes from, and what stays in it. For example, bronze is the cause of a statue in this sense. Silver is the cause of a silver bowl. So are the broader kinds that bronze and silver belong to, such as "metal."

**Second: the formal cause.** This is the form or pattern of a thing. In other words, it is the definition of what the thing is. It also includes the broader kinds that this pattern belongs to, and the parts that make up the definition. For example, the formal cause of a musical octave is the ratio 2 to 1. [Two notes an octave apart are in a 2-to-1 ratio. One string vibrates twice as fast as the other.] More broadly, its formal cause is number.

**Third: the efficient cause.** This is where a change, or a stop to a change, first comes from. For example, a person who gives advice is a cause in this sense. A father is the cause of his child. In general, this is whatever *makes* the thing that gets made, or *changes* the thing that gets changed.

**Fourth: the final cause.** This is the goal: what something is done *for*. For example, health is the cause of walking around. We ask, "Why is he walking around?" We answer, "To be healthy." Once we say that, we think we have given the cause.

The same is true of all the steps along the way. These are steps that something else brings about, as ways to reach the goal. Take the steps toward health: losing weight, purging the body [clearing it out, a common ancient treatment], medicines, and surgical tools. All of them are *for the sake of* health. But they differ from each other. Some are actions, like losing weight. Others are tools, like surgical instruments.

So that is perhaps every way the word "cause" is used.

**One thing can have several causes**

Since "cause" has several meanings, one thing can have several causes. This isn't just because of some side feature of the thing. Take a statue. Both the sculptor's skill and the bronze are causes of it. They cause it *as a statue*, not as anything else it happens to be. But they cause it in different ways. The bronze is the cause as the material. The skill is the cause as the source of the change.

Two things can even cause each other. Hard work causes fitness, and fitness causes hard work. But again, they do it in different ways. One is the goal. The other is where the change starts.

The same thing can also cause opposite results. Sometimes a thing's presence brings about one result. Then we blame its absence for the opposite result. For example, a ship's pilot being on board kept the ship safe. So when the ship is wrecked, we blame the pilot's absence.

**The four causes, in examples**

All the causes we have mentioned fall into four familiar groups.

Look at these pairs:
- letters are the cause of syllables;
- materials are the cause of things people make;
- fire, earth, and the other elements are the cause of physical bodies;
- parts are the cause of the whole;
- the starting assumptions of an argument are the cause of its conclusion.

In each pair, the first thing is a cause in the sense of "what it comes from."

Within these pairs, some causes are the underlying stuff, like the parts. Others are causes as the essence: the whole, the way things are put together, and the form.

Then there are causes like the seed, the doctor, the adviser, and in general anyone who makes something. All of these are where a change, or a state of rest, begins.

The rest are causes as the goal: the good that the other things aim at. "What something is for" means the best thing, the end point that the other steps lead to. (It makes no difference here whether we say "the real good" or "what seems good.")

So those are the kinds of cause, and how many there are.

**The ways a cause can be described**

There are many ways to describe a cause. But they can be sorted into a few groups.

**1. More general versus more specific.** "Cause" is used in many senses. Even within one kind of cause, one description can rank before another. For example, both "the doctor" and "the expert" are causes of health. Both "the ratio 2 to 1" and "number" are causes of the octave. [A doctor is one kind of expert. The ratio 2 to 1 is one kind of number.] The more general description always ranks before the more specific one.

**2. Coincidental causes.** Something can also be called a cause because it just happens to go along with the real cause. The broader kinds it belongs to count too.

Take the statue again. In one sense, "the sculptor" is its cause. In another sense, "Polyclitus" is. [Polyclitus was a famous Greek sculptor.] That's because being Polyclitus just happens to go together with being a sculptor.

The broader groups that include this coincidental feature also count. So we could say "a man" is the cause of the statue, or, more generally, "a living creature."

A coincidental feature can also be closer or further from the real cause. Suppose someone said "the pale man" or "the musical man" was the cause of the statue. [Polyclitus happens to be pale, or musical. That has nothing to do with why the statue exists, so it is a far-off way to name its cause.]

**3. Able to act versus acting now.** Every cause, whether real or coincidental, can be described in two ways: as *able* to act, or as *acting right now*. For example, the cause of a house being built is either "a house-builder" or "a house-builder who is building."

**4. The same applies to effects.** We can make the same distinctions for the things that are caused. For example, the cause can be of "this statue," of "a statue," or of "an image" in general. Or of "this bronze," of "bronze," or of "material" in general. The same goes for coincidental features.

**5. Combined descriptions.** We can also combine descriptions, for causes or effects. For example, instead of saying "Polyclitus" or "the sculptor," we can say "Polyclitus the sculptor."

**Adding it up**

All these ways come to six. Each of the six can then be taken in two ways. A cause can be described as:
1. the specific thing;
2. the broader kind it belongs to;
3. a coincidental feature;
4. the broader kind of that feature;
5. a combination of these;
6. one of these on its own.

And each of the six can be taken as *acting now* or as *able to act*.

**How "acting now" differs from "able to act"**

Here is the difference. A specific cause that is acting right now begins and ends at the same time as its effect. For example, "this person healing" exists only as long as "this person being healed." "That man building a house" exists only as long as "that house being built."

This is not always true of causes that are only *able* to act. For example, a house and its builder don't die at the same time.

**Always look for the most exact cause**

When we look for the cause of anything, we should always look for the most exact one. That holds in other studies too. For example, why does a man build? Because he is a builder. And a builder builds because of his building skill. So that last cause, the skill, comes first. The same holds in general.

**Match the cause to the effect**

General effects go with general causes, and specific effects go with specific causes. For example, "a statue" goes with "a sculptor," and "this statue" goes with "this sculptor."

In the same way, abilities go with things that *could* happen. Causes acting right now go with things that *are* happening right now.

**Conclusion**

That is enough to explain how many causes there are, and the ways something can be a cause.
